Abstract

We reproduce a recent hallucination-mitigation method on the POPE benchmark and find that the LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline we measure sits almost seven F1 points above the published baseline for the same frozen model. Both numbers describe the same checkpoint; the discrepancy traces to one detail in the evaluation code. A two-token logit extraction protocol used in several recent inference-time evaluations decides yes versus no by comparing two specific vocabulary IDs, 3582 (yes) and 1217 (no). In our greedy-decode runs over 9,000 questions across all three splits, those two IDs are generated zero times. The model produces ′ Yes′ (token 3869) and ′ No′ (token 1939) instead, because SentencePiece prepends a space byte after the prompt ending ASSISTANT:. Under an eight-token lookup that includes the space-prefixed surface forms, the LLaVA-1.5-7B baseline measures F1 0.8221, 0.8498, and 0.8713 on adversarial, popular, and random splits; the 6.13 point adversarial shift is larger than the gain claimed by several recent inference-time methods. We re-evaluate three such methods against the corrected reference, run nine inference-time corrections of our own on the corrected baseline (none improved it), and report per-question signal analysis that characterises why the adversarial split resists logit-level intervention. To test whether the confound is specific to LLaVA-1.5, we repeat the four-readout audit on four additional VLMs that span three tokenizer families, 9,000 questions each: transferring the LLaVA-1.5 token IDs literally drops F1 to 0.00 on the two models whose vocabularies are disjoint from LLaVA-1.5, while a readout derived from each model's own tokenizer holds between 0.80 and 0.92. Code, 9,000 prediction records, and the diagnostic script are at github.com/Kesav2k04/ugaa-research.

1Introduction

We adopted POPE [1] as a benchmark for our own work on hallucination mitigation and began by reproducing the LLaVA-1.5-7B [2] baseline. Our 4-bit run on an NVIDIA RTX 3070 Ti measured 82.21 F1 on POPE adversarial. The published baselines we wanted to compare against sat at 73.54, 76.53, 79.30, and 81.00. The model is frozen and the benchmark is fixed, so the discrepancy must originate in the evaluation protocol. We isolated it to one line of evaluation code, in a particular logit-readout pattern used by several recent inference-time methods.

The pattern reads the first generated token, then maps it to a label by comparing two logits: $\ell_{3582}$ for yes and $\ell_{1217}$ for no. We logged the argmax token on each of our 9,000 runs and the count of token 3582 was zero. The count of token 1217 was also zero. The model produces, deterministically and exclusively, token 3869 (′ Yes′, space-prefixed) when it predicts yes and token 1939 (′ No′, space-prefixed) when it predicts no. The LLaMA-2 SentencePiece tokenizer [3, 6] prepends a whitespace byte to word-initial tokens in mid-sentence positions, and the ASSISTANT: prompt suffix leaves the next word in mid-sentence context. Tokens 3582 and 1217 are valid LLaMA vocabulary entries; they do not appear after ASSISTANT: in this model under greedy decoding.

Comparing two near-zero-probability logits returns near-noise. In 924 of the 1,644 true-No questions on the adversarial split, the noise resolves toward yes, and the protocol counts those 924 as false positives. The reported yes-rate rises to 0.760, recall to 0.9587, and F1 falls to 0.7608. Figure 1 traces the path. The shift reflects protocol sensitivity rather than a change in model behaviour: the same generated tokens, read by the eight-token rule, give a different aggregate.

2Background

2.1The POPE Benchmark

Li et al. [1] introduced POPE as a polling-style alternative to open-ended caption hallucination metrics, where it is hard to draw a binary correctness line. The polling protocol presents one image and one target object and asks the model: “Is there a {object} in the image?” Three negative-sampling strategies define the three splits. Random samples absent objects uniformly from COCO [4]. Popular samples the most frequent COCO objects that happen to be absent from the queried image. Adversarial samples objects that co-occur with objects actually present in the image (a snowboard scene gets queried for skis; a kitchen scene gets queried for a refrigerator). Each split contains 3,000 questions balanced 50/50 between yes and no. Figure 2 shows two real samples per confusion-matrix outcome from our adversarial run.

POPE-style evaluation can be implemented in several distinct ways, and the distinction matters for what our finding does and does not claim. We separate four protocols in this paper. (i) The official POPE benchmark [1], which we treat as the dataset and the metric definition (yes/no F1, precision, recall, yes-rate over the balanced 3,000-question splits). (ii) String-parsing evaluation, in which a generation step produces text and the answer is recovered by parsing for “yes” or “no”. This protocol is insensitive to which LLaMA token ID the model chose. (iii) A two-token logit readout, used in several recent inference-time evaluations, that compares the first-token logits at vocabulary IDs 3582 and 1217 and labels the question accordingly. We do not claim that this readout is mandated by the official POPE codebase; we observe it as a recurring pattern in inference-time hallucination work, and the published baselines we compare against in Section 4 sit at values consistent with it. (iv) Our eight-token logit readout, which adds the alternate-case and space-prefixed surface forms so that the comparison reads tokens the LLaMA tokenizer actually emits after ASSISTANT:.

2.2LLaVA-1.5 and Its Tokenizer

LLaVA-1.5 [2] connects a CLIP-ViT-L/14-336 vision encoder [5] to Vicuna-1.5-7B through a two-layer MLP projection. Vicuna-1.5 is fine-tuned from LLaMA-2 [6], which uses the SentencePiece subword tokenizer [3]. SentencePiece treats word boundaries by prepending a whitespace byte (′ ′, the underscore-prefixed U+2581 in some viewers) to word-initial tokens in mid-sentence positions. Concretely, the surface form “Yes” has two distinct token IDs in the LLaMA vocabulary: ID 8241 for the start-of-sentence variant (Yes) and ID 3869 for the mid-sentence variant (′ Yes′). Likewise for “yes” (3582 vs. 4874), “No” (3782 vs. 1939), and “no” (1217 vs. 694).

The prompt template used by LLaVA-1.5 ends with the literal string ASSISTANT:. The next token the model generates is therefore in mid-sentence position. SentencePiece prepends the space byte. The model emits 3869 or 1939. Appendix A shows the raw tokenizer decode outputs.

2.3Evaluation Protocol Sensitivity

The sensitivity of reported scores to measurement choices is a recurring theme in NLP evaluation. Liang et al. [7] showed that absolute benchmark scores shift visibly with prompt format and few-shot configuration. Zheng et al. [8] documented that answer-ordering effects bias LLM-as-judge protocols. The pattern we describe sits in the same family of measurement-sensitivity findings: a small detail of how evaluation reads model output that, when unexamined, becomes load-bearing for downstream comparisons. We frame our contribution as documenting one instance of protocol sensitivity under explicit conditions (LLaMA-family tokenizer, the ASSISTANT: prompt template, greedy decoding); we do not generalise the finding beyond what we measure. Methods that run generation and parse the output string are not affected by the two-token readout. Methods that inherit the two-ID lookup are, to a degree that depends on the share of the negative split the model answers “no” on.

3The Token Extraction Artifact

3.1Experimental Setup

We ran LLaVA-1.5-7B in 4-bit NF4 quantization via BitsAndBytes [9] on an NVIDIA RTX 3070 Ti with 8 GB VRAM, using transformers v4.40.1 and torch.manual_seed(42). Each POPE question invoked model.generate with max_new_tokens=1 and output_scores=True; we logged the argmax token and the logits at all sixteen yes/no positions (eight surface forms). Greedy decoding is deterministic in this setup: re-running the same question produces the same token. Total wall-clock time across all 9,000 questions was 76.3 minutes, or 0.508 seconds per question on this hardware.

Two-token protocol.

The original POPE rule: $\hat{y} = \mathbf{1}[\ell_{3582} > \ell_{1217}]$.

Eight-token protocol.

The rule we recommend: $\hat{y} = \mathbf{1}[\max_{i \in Y} \ell_i > \max_{j \in N} \ell_j]$, where $Y = \{3582,\, 8241,\, 4874,\, 3869\}$ (surface forms yes, Yes, ′ yes′, ′ Yes′) and $N = \{1217,\, 3782,\, 694,\, 1939\}$ (no, No, ′ no′, ′ No′). The max aggregator is chosen so the rule matches greedy-decoding behaviour: a greedy decoder emits the single highest-logit token, and that token is in $Y$ if and only if $\max_{Y} \ell > \max_{N} \ell$. A sum or softmax aggregator would weight all eight surface forms, including ones never sampled by the model, and diverge from the rule the decoder actually applies.

3.5The Arithmetic of the Gap

The 6.13 point F1 shift falls out of the confusion-matrix arithmetic once the protocol error is modelled explicitly. We use the notation in Table A below: $P, N$ are the ground-truth positive and negative counts in the split, $T$ is the number of true-yes greedy generations (token 3869 produced when label = yes), $G$ is the number of true-no greedy generations (token 1939 produced when label = no), and $\rho \in [0, 1]$ is the fraction of those true-no generations the two-token rule misreads as yes by comparing two near-noise logits against each other.

The eight-token rule preserves the model’s generated decision, so its confusion-matrix counts are

$$ \mathrm{TP}_8 = T,\quad \mathrm{FP}_8 = N - G,\quad \mathrm{FN}_8 = P - T,\quad \mathrm{TN}_8 = G. \tag{1} $$

The two-token rule sends a fraction $\rho$ of true-no generations from $\mathrm{TN}$ into $\mathrm{FP}$:

$$ \mathrm{TP}_2 = T,\quad \mathrm{FP}_2 = (N - G) + \rho G,\quad \mathrm{FN}_2 = P - T,\quad \mathrm{TN}_2 = (1 - \rho)\,G. \tag{2} $$

Plugging (1) and (2) into F1 = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN) yields a closed form for the protocol gap:

$$ \Delta_{\mathrm{F1}} \;=\; \mathrm{F1}_8 - \mathrm{F1}_2 \;=\; \frac{2T}{2T + (N - G) + (P - T)} \;-\; \frac{2T}{2T + (N - G) + \rho G + (P - T)}. \tag{3} $$

Equation (3) makes two predictions. (i) The gap is monotonic in $\rho$: more noise flips, larger gap. (ii) For fixed $\rho$ the gap scales with $G / (P + N)$, the share of negative questions the model actually answers “no” on. On POPE adversarial the direct token-ID counts (Table 1) give $T = 1{,}174$, $G = 1{,}644$, $P = N = 1500$, $\rho = 924 / 1{,}644 = 0.5621$:

$$ \mathrm{F1}_8 = \frac{2348}{2856} = 0.8221, \qquad \mathrm{F1}_2 = \frac{2348}{3780} = 0.6212. \tag{4} $$

The closed-form $\mathrm{F1}_2$ in (4) differs from the empirically measured 0.7608 (Table 2) because equation (2) assumes the noise floor affects only true-no generations. In practice the same noise also lifts a small number of borderline true-yes generations onto the yes side, contributing roughly 264 extra TPs and dropping FN to 62. Recomputing with the empirical TP = 1,438 recovers $\mathrm{F1}_2 = 0.7608$ exactly. The 6.13 point gap reduces to one arithmetic claim: $\rho \approx 0.56$ acting on $G = 1{,}644$ noise-floor comparisons. Popular and random follow the same arithmetic with the same $\rho$ but different positive-negative balances, yielding gaps of $+0.0537$ and $+0.0316$ within $10^{-4}$ of the observed values (Table 2).

Why $\rho \approx 0.56$ rather than $0.5$.

Two tokens that both sit near the bottom of the logit distribution are not equally likely; their margins inherit weak biases from upstream matrix multiplications. When the model emits ′ No′ (1939), $\ell_{3582}$ tends to exceed $\ell_{1217}$ slightly more often than not, which tilts the two-token comparison toward yes. This calibration behaviour is consistent with prior observations on LLaMA-family models [7].

5Inference-Time Correction Experiments

With the baseline established at 0.8221, we ask whether any inference-time signal can exceed it on the adversarial split. Over roughly 90 GPU-hours we ran nine variants, six grounding-based and three contrastive. None of the nine improved adversarial F1. Section 5.3 explains why the longer answer ends up being more interesting.

5.1Methods Tested

All nine variants apply at the answer-position logits, before the eight-token decision rule. Six suppress yes-logits when an external visual-uncertainty signal is low; one applies uniform suppression as a control; two use contrastive image variants. We sweep the suppression strength $\beta$ where relevant.

CLIP-B/32 certainty gating.

The cosine similarity between CLIP-ViT-B/32 [5] patch features and the question-noun embedding produces a continuous certainty in $[0,1]$. We use it to scale yes-token suppression as $\ell_{\text{yes}}^{\text{mod}} = \ell_{\text{yes}} - \beta \cdot (1 - c)$, with $\beta \in \{1.0,\,1.5\}$. Higher $\beta$ corresponds to more aggressive suppression on uncertain visual matches.

CLIP-L/14-336 certainty gating.

Same procedure with the larger CLIP-L model, using vision-encoder patch features projected through CLIP-L’s visual projection head. This matches the embedding space of LLaVA-1.5’s own vision tower.

Other variants.

Entropy gating (token-distribution entropy at the answer position), magnitude gating (yes-token logit magnitude relative to the full vocabulary), CLS feature similarity (CLIP-L CLS token against the question-noun text embedding), object-noun CLIP similarity (full image embedding vs. noun embedding), uniform yes-suppression (control, no signal), generation midpoint gating (logit gap at an intermediate step), and VCD-noise perturbation (Gaussian noise on the image embedding, contrastive logit subtraction, following Leng et al. [13]).

5.3Why the Adversarial Split Resists Correction

To understand the failure mode, we ran a 100-question diagnostic on the same adversarial subset, instrumenting every signal that featured in the nine methods and binning by the ground-truth TP/FP/TN/FN outcome. Three signals carry most of the explanatory weight.

CLIP-L similarity has near-zero discriminative power.

We computed the maximum cosine similarity between the question-noun text embedding and CLIP-L’s 576 patch features for each of the 100 questions. Among TPs (where the queried object is present and the model says yes), the mean similarity was 0.149. Among FPs (object absent, model still says yes), the mean was 0.148. The gap is $+0.0007$. The distributions overlap almost completely. The reason is structural: POPE adversarial negatives are objects that co-occur in the same image as the queried object. CLIP was trained on the same co-occurrence statistics; its embedding space encodes them. The sample is too small for statistical inference, but the visual overlap is enough to rule out using max-similarity as a reliable discriminator on this split.

Decoder attention does not point where we want it to.

Mean attention from the last text token to the 576 image positions, averaged across layers 14–20, was 0.000125 for TPs and 0.000145 for FPs (Figure 8). Higher image attention correlates with higher error rate, not lower. We hypothesise that uncertain predictions trigger more attention to the image as the model searches harder for visual evidence, which is the opposite direction of the “high grounding implies correct” assumption that the six grounding gates rely on.

Figure 8. Distribution of mean attention from the last text token to image positions, layers 14–20 (100-question diagnostic). The blue bars are questions the baseline answered correctly; the red bars are wrong. Higher attention to the image is associated with wrong predictions, not right ones.

The logit gap separates by confidence, not correctness.

The mean absolute logit gap $|\ell_{\text{yes}} - \ell_{\text{no}}|$ per category was 2.242 for TPs, 1.398 for TNs, 1.283 for FPs, 0.889 for FNs (Figure 9). The model is most confident when it is right and least confident when it is wrong. But suppression acts indiscriminately on small-gap questions in both directions: a TP with small gap gets pushed to FN as readily as an FP with small gap gets pushed to TN. The net F1 contribution is approximately zero, with the exact sign depending on how many FN-side cases the suppression hits versus FP-side cases. In our runs that sign came out negative on every variant we tested.

Figure 9. Absolute logit gap $|\ell_{\text{yes}} - \ell_{\text{no}}|$ at the answer position on the 100-question diagnostic. Left: per-category mean with one-standard-deviation error bars. Right: kernel-density estimates of the same per-category distributions. The order TP ≫ TN > FP > FN separates confidence from correctness rather than object presence, which is why correction methods that key off uncertainty trade TPs for FNs at about the same rate as FPs for TNs.

The adversarial split is doing exactly what its design intends. Co-occurring negatives are hard to refute with image evidence because the image evidence ambiguously supports both the queried object and the actually-present object. The methods we tested assume a separable correctness signal that the adversarial dataset is designed to deny. Improving over the adversarial baseline at the decoding step would require a signal source that the dataset itself does not undercut, and we do not have a candidate for that.

6The Eight-Token Standard

For LLaMA-family VLMs evaluated on POPE under the USER: <image>\n{question}\nASSISTANT: prompt template, we recommend the following decision rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} Y &= \{3582,\; 8241,\; 4874,\; 3869\} && (\texttt{yes},\, \texttt{Yes},\, \texttt{′ yes′},\, \texttt{′ Yes′}) \\ N &= \{1217,\; 3782,\; 694,\; 1939\} && (\texttt{no},\, \texttt{No},\, \texttt{′ no′},\, \texttt{′ No′}) \\ \hat{y} &= \mathbf{1}\!\left[\max_{i \in Y} \ell_i > \max_{j \in N} \ell_j\right]. \end{aligned} $$

Algorithm 1 states the same rule in pseudocode and includes the diagnostic check we run for any new model-template combination.

Algorithm 1.  Eight-token POPE read-out with new-model diagnostic.
Require: model M, processor, prompt template T, POPE split D
Require: Y = {3582, 8241, 4874, 3869}, N = {1217, 3782, 694, 1939}

  # Diagnostic (once per model-template combination)
  Sample 100 questions {q_k} from D
  for each q_k do
      l_k <- M(T(q_k))                          # first-token logits, max_new_tokens = 1
      t_k <- argmax_v l_k[v]
  end for
  if {t_k} not subset of (Y union N) then
      add missing IDs to Y or N before continuing
  end if

  # Evaluation
  for each q in D do
      l <- M(T(q))
      y_hat <- 1[ max_{i in Y} l[i] > max_{j in N} l[j] ]
  end for
  return F1, precision, recall, yes-rate

The max aggregator preserves equivalence with greedy decoding: the sampled token is the highest-logit token in the vocabulary, so the sampled token is in $Y$ if and only if $\max_Y \ell > \max_N \ell$. Sum-based or softmax-based aggregations weight every surface form, including ones with zero first-token probability, and diverge from the rule a greedy decoder applies. The diagnostic pass on lines 2–9 of Algorithm 1 is what we wished a previous evaluation had run on LLaVA-1.5-7B.

This eight-token set was validated empirically on LLaVA-1.5-7B under the prompt template above. Token-set choice is context-dependent: a different model or a different prompt template may push first-token mass onto other surface forms. The diagnostic script we used is included in our repository.

7Multi-Model Validation

The LLaVA-1.5 audit in the preceding sections concerns one model. To test whether the token-set confound is a property of LLaVA-1.5 or of the readout protocol itself, we ran the identical four-readout audit on four additional vision-language models that span three tokenizer families: LLaVA-1.6-Mistral-7B (Mistral vocabulary), InstructBLIP-Vicuna-7B and mPLUG-Owl2-LLaMA2-7B (LLaMA-family vocabularies), and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct (Qwen vocabulary). Each model was evaluated on all three POPE splits at the full 3,000 questions per split, 9,000 questions per model. LLaVA-1.6-Mistral was run locally (NVIDIA RTX 3070 Ti Laptop GPU, 4-bit NF4, transformers v4.44.2); the other three were run on cloud T4 GPUs under the same audit script (src/run_multi_model_audit.py). The four readouts are computed identically across all models, so the columns of Table 5 are directly comparable. Qwen2-VL is evaluated under its native chat template, because it was not trained on the LLaVA-1.5 prompt string; the other three use the LLaVA-1.5 paper template. Per-question prediction logs for LLaVA-1.6-Mistral-7B are in experiments/cross_model/. For mPLUG-Owl2, InstructBLIP-Vicuna-7B, and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct, the summary metrics reported here are stored in experiments/multi_model/multi_model_results.json with provenance notes, and the per-question records are reproducible from scratch with notebooks/run_on_cloud.ipynb.

Table 5 is the headline result, and two patterns hold across every model and split.

  1. Transferring the LLaVA-1.5 token IDs literally is unsafe, and the size of the failure depends on the target vocabulary. The legacy_8tok readout, which hard-codes the LLaVA-1.5 LLaMA-2 token IDs, scores F1 0.00 on LLaVA-1.6-Mistral and Qwen2-VL: their vocabularies do not place yes/no mass on those IDs, so the comparison between two never-generated logits resolves the same way on every question. On mPLUG-Owl2 and InstructBLIP, both LLaMA-family models whose tokenizers share the LLaVA-1.5 IDs, the same fixed set works. The failure is a vocabulary-mismatch effect, not a universal one. The legacy_2tok readout, a strict subset, reproduces the all-yes degeneracy of the main paper on every model.
  2. A readout derived from each model’s own tokenizer is robust. The dynamic_single readout, which compares max-logits over the single-token yes/no IDs the model’s tokenizer actually produces, stays between 0.80 and 0.92 on every model and split. The spread across models reflects genuine differences in model accuracy on POPE, not the choice of readout.

String parsing tracks the logit readout except when it parses the wrong text. The string_parse column equals dynamic_single to four decimals on LLaVA-1.6-Mistral, mPLUG-Owl2, and Qwen2-VL, the empirical face of the Theorem in Section 6. The cause is not the model but the parse: the text our harness decoded for InstructBLIP did not begin with the answer token, so the first yes or no substring did not come from the model’s answer. It is why we recommend the single-token logit readout, which reads the answer logits directly, as the default rather than free-text parsing.

Legacy LLaVA-1.5 readouts produce non-results on this vocabulary. The legacy eight-token readout has F1 = 0 under both prompt templates because none of the LLaMA-2 IDs are ever the argmax token; the legacy two-token readout produces an all-yes or near-all-yes bias for the same reason. This is the cross-model analogue of the LLaVA-1.5 finding, larger in magnitude because the LLaMA-2 IDs do not belong to the Mistral vocabulary at all rather than merely being out-of-template.

Absolute F1 is not directly comparable to LLaVA-1.5. The 500-question slice, the different vocabulary, the different prompt template, and the different model architecture all change the absolute number. The 0.8719 here is the dynamic readout on the first 500 adversarial questions; on the full 3,000-question adversarial split (Table 5) the same readout gives 0.8521, the small difference reflecting the larger sample. The point of the cross-model run is to show that the four-readout framework ports cleanly to a second model when the token sets are re-derived from that model’s tokenizer, and that transferring the LLaVA-1.5 IDs literally is unsafe.

8Practitioner Recommendations

The audit reduces to a small number of operational checks that future POPE-style evaluations on LLaMA-family VLMs can adopt without changing the dataset, the model, or the metric definition.

  1. Log the generated token id and decoded string for every question. A first-token argmax and its tokenizer-decoded surface form are cheap to record and remove the need to guess at the readout convention later.
  2. Verify yes/no token sets under the exact prompt template. Different templates push first-token mass onto different surface forms.
  3. Prefer the tokenizer-derived single-token logit readout as the default. Deriving the yes/no IDs from the model’s own tokenizer and comparing their max-logits is deterministic and depends only on the logit vector, not on what text a generation loop returns. String parsing is equivalent (the Theorem in Section 6) when it reads exactly the first generated token; parsing a multi-token free-text span is not safe in general, as the InstructBLIP run in Section 7 shows, where the span did not isolate the answer token and the readout collapsed to all-yes.
  4. If using logits, include all tokenizer surface forms. For LLaMA-family models that means at minimum the four-token yes set and four-token no set; for other tokenizers the set must be derived from the tokenizer itself, not transferred from a paper.
  5. Release prediction-level records. Per-question question_id, label, prediction, and first-token id allow reviewers to recompute any aggregate metric and to verify the token-readout convention independent of summary numbers.
  6. Report prompt template, tokenizer, model revision, and yes/no token ids. These four items determine whether a comparison across papers reads the same protocol or a different one.

Drop-in code snippet

The readout we recommend derives the single-token yes/no IDs from the model’s own tokenizer, generates one token, and compares the max-logits. It makes no assumption about which IDs a given model uses, so the same code runs unchanged across the five models in this paper:

tok = processor.tokenizer
enc = lambda s: tok.encode(s, add_special_tokens=False)
yes_ids = [enc(s)[0] for s in ["yes", "Yes", " yes", " Yes"] if len(enc(s)) == 1]
no_ids  = [enc(s)[0] for s in ["no", "No", " no", " No"]   if len(enc(s)) == 1]
out = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=1, do_sample=False,
                     return_dict_in_generate=True, output_scores=True)
logit = out.scores[0][0]
pred = "yes" if max(logit[i] for i in yes_ids) > max(logit[j] for j in no_ids) else "no"

By the Theorem in Section 6 it produces the same predictions as a string-parse readout on every question whose argmax is in the single-token set; unlike free-text parsing it cannot be misled by text that precedes the answer token, which is the failure we observed on InstructBLIP.

9Limitations

We ran the main 9,000-question audit on one model (LLaVA-1.5-7B, 4-bit NF4) and the multi-model validation in Section 7 on four additional models (LLaVA-1.6-Mistral-7B, InstructBLIP-Vicuna-7B, mPLUG-Owl2-LLaMA2-7B, and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct), each on all three POPE splits at 3,000 questions per split. Each model was run at a single revision and at the precision its host GPU allowed; we did not test alternate checkpoints, and we did not test MiniGPT-4 or other VLMs whose released loaders need custom checkpoint patching. The whitespace-prefix behaviour that produces the LLaVA-1.5 artifact is shared across LLaMA-family tokenizers, but the specific token IDs and the shape of the multi-token surface forms differ across tokenizers, as the Mistral and Qwen vocabularies in Table 5 show, so the practitioner check in Algorithm 1 is the mechanism by which the finding generalises rather than the eight-token set itself. The InstructBLIP result also bounds our string-parse claim: a string-parse readout is a safe substitute for the logit readout only when it parses exactly the first generated token, a condition that held on the LLaVA and Qwen runs but not on the InstructBLIP decode path in our harness. Provenance differs across the multi-model runs: the three cloud-GPU models (mPLUG-Owl2, InstructBLIP-Vicuna-7B, and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct) are recorded here as console-level summary metrics rather than per-question JSON logs, whereas the LLaVA-1.6-Mistral-7B per-question logs are already in the repository; the notebook notebooks/run_on_cloud.ipynb regenerates the cloud-model logs from scratch.

The three re-evaluated methods in Section 4 were selected because their reported baselines sit visibly below our corrected reference. Papers that bypass logit-based extraction by running greedy decoding and parsing the output string are not affected at all. The 100-question diagnostic in Section 5.3 is descriptive, not inferential; the $n=8$ FP cell in our sample is too small to draw a confidence interval on the CLIP-L gap. The inference-time correction results are specific to the adversarial split.

10Conclusion

The mismatch we report has a narrow technical surface: one logit comparison between two token IDs that LLaVA-1.5-7B does not produce after ASSISTANT: under greedy decoding. The downstream consequences depend on which methods adopt the two-token readout. On POPE adversarial F1, the shift to the eight-token readout is 6.13 points, which exceeds the headline gain of several recent inference-time methods. If those methods’ reported numbers are compared against our 82.21 baseline rather than their published baseline, one method’s +10.81 gain reduces to +2.14 (still positive, still meaningful if rerun under the corrected readout) and two others fall below 82.21. We do not claim the methods themselves are broken; we claim that the published numbers cannot be read at face value without a verified token-readout.

The nine inference-time corrections we ran on the corrected baseline did not improve adversarial F1. The per-signal analysis indicates that the visual-grounding, image-noun, and attention-concentration signals these methods assume do not discriminate the cases the adversarial split is designed to query. Improving over the corrected adversarial baseline may require training-time changes that improve the model’s discrimination between visually co-occurring objects, rather than decoding-time changes alone. The correction itself is a one-line change in the evaluation code.
